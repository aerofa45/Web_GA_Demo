Idea A · Step 1

The robot that cannot stay in one place

First understand the mission, not the theorem.

1

Imagine a robot with two jobs

Every morning it measures an environmental region. Then it travels to a servicing region to recharge. Then it returns to measure again. The robot must alternate between two areas that do not overlap.

This is alternating behavior—not a robot frozen at one point.

Our question: Does the robot eventually settle into a stable repeating pair of positions?

Idea A · Step 2

Make the robot move using real numbers

No calculus needed: only a number line and fractions.

2

Define the two regions

A = [0, 1]    and    B = [2, 3] These are all points between 0 and 1, and all points between 2 and 3.

The nearest two positions are 1 and 2. Therefore the minimum possible travel distance is D = 2 − 1 = 1.

3

Choose a movement rule

For teaching, we invent a simple rule. A position in A maps to B; a position in B maps back to A:

T(x) = 2 + (1 − x)/2   if x ∈ A
T(x) = 1 − (x − 2)/2   if x ∈ B T(x) means “next position.” This rule is an illustrative example, not Kim's robotics algorithm.

Start at 0. Compute the next positions using ordinary algebra:

Notice the pattern. The robot keeps traveling, but the distances 2.5 → 1.75 → 1.375 → 1.1875 approach 1. The stable result is a two-point cycle: 1 → 2 → 1 → 2.

Idea A · Step 3

How this connects to our theorem

Now introduce the mathematical meaning, one symbol at a time.

4

Fixed point versus best proximity point

A fixed point means a rule does not move a position: T(x*) = x*. Our robot cannot have one, because every A position must map into B.

A best proximity point is different. It asks for the smallest possible movement between the disjoint sets:

d(x*, T(x*)) = dist(A, B)

Here d(x,y)=|x−y| is ordinary distance. We found x*=1, because T(1)=2 and |1−2|=1.

5

What does “contraction” mean?

Think of an error getting smaller each time. If the extra distance beyond the minimum gap is initially 1.5, then multiplying it by 0.5 repeatedly gives 0.75, 0.375, 0.1875, and so on.

d(Tx, Ty) ≤ k · d(x, y) + (1−k) · D Here k is between 0 and 1, and D is the minimum gap between the regions.

For our example, we can verify k = 0.5. For x in A and y in B:

d(Tx,Ty) = 0.5 + 0.5 · d(x,y)

Therefore, whenever the current distance is d, its excess above the minimum becomes half as large.

6

Where our integral-type result fits

Our published paper generalizes a distance contraction with an integral control function φ(t):

∫₀d(Tx,Ty) φ(t)dt  ≤  k∫₀d(x,y) φ(t)dt + (1−k)∫₀D φ(t)dt

If φ(t)=1, an integral is just the area of a rectangle: ∫₀ʳ 1dt = r. So the advanced expression reduces to the simple contraction inequality above.

Research caution: A decreasing distance between consecutive iterates does not automatically prove that an entire robot trajectory is stable. Extra assumptions and proofs are needed to establish convergence of robot positions or task assignments.
Idea A · Step 4

Run the arithmetic in Python

This is a direct reproduction of the movement example, not yet a robot controller.

Python · cyclic_example.py
def next_position(x):
    if 0 <= x <= 1:         # Region A → B
        return 2 + (1 - x) / 2
    elif 2 <= x <= 3:       # Region B → A
        return 1 - (x - 2) / 2
    raise ValueError("Outside regions A and B")

x = 0.0
for step in range(1, 9):
    x_new = next_position(x)
    distance = abs(x_new - x)
    print(step, round(x_new, 6), round(distance, 6))
    x = x_new
Connection to our paper: This illustrates a cyclic map, best proximity point, and contraction factor. our publication addresses more general integral-type contractions and mathematical conditions for proximity behavior.
Idea A · Step 5

Turn the teaching example into a real research question

Once the simple example makes sense, replace a single position with a multi-robot state.

7

Represent multiple robots and multiple objectives

Suppose three robots have positions (x₁,y₁), (x₂,y₂), (x₃,y₃). Their combined state is X. Their mission cost could contain three terms:

J(X) = α·TravelCost(X) + β·EnergyCost(X) + γ·MissedDetections(X)

The Greek letters α, β, γ are weights showing what the system prioritizes. For example, a high γ makes missed detections particularly expensive.

8

Define a real alternating task-update rule

Now A and B would represent feasible sets of whole-team configurations, not just intervals on a line. Define a rule that sends a sensing assignment to a servicing assignment and back while respecting energy, communication, and movement constraints.

Xₙ₊₁ = T(Xₙ)

Then attempt to prove that the chosen T has useful convergence or best-proximity properties. Compare mission quality and stability against standard assignment methods.

Possible Ph.D. contribution: A feasible alternating task-allocation algorithm with a rigorously proved convergence property and evidence that it improves an operational objective. This has not yet been implemented or proved.

Idea B · Step 1

Where should we put the sensors?

First solve the environment. Then decide how to measure it.

1

Imagine an underground water channel

Water flows through two soil layers. One is harder for water to pass through; the other is more permeable. We have only two sensors but several places where you could install them.

There are two different questions: What is happening in the water? and Where should the sensors go? Our groundwater PINN addresses the first; Kim's sensor-placement mathematics addresses the second.

A simplified 1D environment. The water model alone does not determine sensor detection probability.
Idea B · Step 2

Solve groundwater flow with simple algebra

First solve a tiny case exactly, so we know what a PINN should learn.

2

Choose a few easy numbers

Hydraulic head at left boundary
10

Each soil section has length 1. The full channel runs from x=0 to x=2. Let H be the unknown head at the middle, x=1.

3

Use the idea of constant flow

In a steady system without sources, the same quantity of water must flow through each section. Darcy's law says flow is conductivity times head drop per unit length:

q = −K · (Δh / Δx)

First soil: q = 1 · (10−H)/1 = 10−H.

Second soil: q = 4 · (H−0)/1 = 4H.

The two flow rates must be equal:

10 − H = 4H
10 = 5H
H = 2 Only one-variable algebra!

Then q = 10−2 = 8. Now we know the head at the left, middle, and right: 10 → 2 → 0.

4

Find the complete head function

Each soil has constant conductivity. Within each section, head changes linearly:

h(x) = 10 − 8x   for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1
h(x) = 4 − 2x   for 1 < x ≤ 2

At x=0.5, head is 10−8(0.5)=6. At x=1.5, it is 4−2(1.5)=1.

Idea B · Step 3

Replace the exact solution with our PINN

This is where our existing research enters.

5

Why use a neural network if algebra works?

For two simple soil sections, algebra is enough. But real groundwater systems have complicated geometry, many conductivity values, uncertain boundaries, and measurements at scattered locations.

A physics-informed neural network predicts the whole head function as hθ(x), where θ means trainable network weights.

q(x) = −K(x) · dh/dx
dq/dx = 0
⇒ d/dx [K(x) · dh/dx] = 0

The first equation is Darcy's law, and the second is conservation. Together they give our governing PDE.

6

Train the network to obey the physics

A PINN creates a residual—the amount by which its prediction violates the equation:

rθ(x) = d/dx [K(x) · dhθ/dx]

It then minimizes both physics and boundary mistakes:

Loss = Mean(physics residual²) + λ · BoundaryError

Our earlier research compared fixed and adaptive boundary-loss weights under heterogeneous conductivity, including 100:1 contrasts. That is genuine experience we could transfer into this project.

Technical detail: At a sharp conductivity boundary, enforce continuity of head and flux. Do not naively differentiate a discontinuous conductivity profile at that interface.
Idea B · Step 4

Now choose the best two sensors

Use probability, multiplication, and addition. This is the bridge to Kim's 2023 sensor-placement paper.

7

Turn environmental conditions into a toy arrival model

For the worked example only, suppose expected target arrivals are described by λ(x)=1+0.2h(x). This is invented to make the numbers easy, not a physical relationship established by our groundwater research.

At the two zone centers:

Zone 1: h(0.5)=6 → λ₁ = 1+0.2(6) = 2.2
Zone 2: h(1.5)=1 → λ₂ = 1+0.2(1) = 1.2

These are average target-arrival counts during a monitoring interval, not percentages.

8

Give each candidate sensor a detection probability

Candidate sensorZone 1Zone 2
A80%20%
B30%70%
C60%60%

Again, these probabilities are teaching assumptions. The real research would need a validated relationship among target location, sensor design, and environmental state.

For a single sensor, expected detected targets = expected arrivals × detection probability, summed over zones.

F(A)=2.2×0.8 + 1.2×0.2 = 2.00
F(B)=2.2×0.3 + 1.2×0.7 = 1.50
F(C)=2.2×0.6 + 1.2×0.6 = 2.04

The best first choice is C, because 2.04 is largest.

9

Why two sensors are not just two single-sensor scores added together

The two sensors may detect the same target. We count that target only once. The easiest approach is to calculate the chance that both sensors miss the target, then subtract from 1.

For C and A in Zone 1:

P(both miss) = (1−0.6)(1−0.8) = 0.4×0.2 = 0.08
P(at least one detects) = 1−0.08 = 0.92

For C and A in Zone 2:

1 − (1−0.6)(1−0.2) = 1−0.32 = 0.68

So their total expected coverage is

F(C,A)=2.2×0.92 + 1.2×0.68 = 2.84

Similarly, F(C,B)=2.64. Therefore the best second choice after C is A.

Result: Pick sensors C and A. In this example they cover an expected 2.84 of the 3.4 target arrivals.

Idea B · Step 5

What happens if the environment is uncertain?

Here our Heston experience and Kim's 2026 Gamma–Laplace paper become relevant.

10

Do not confuse an average with a random quantity

Suppose one zone has an average of two targets, but the actual arrival intensity changes from day to day. Replacing the uncertain intensity by the number 2 loses information about variability.

Under a Poisson arrival model, if expected missed arrivals are m, the chance of zero missed arrivals is e−m.

If the mean intensity is 2 and the miss probability is 0.4, the mean-only calculation is:

e−2×0.4 = e−0.8 ≈ 0.4493 (44.93%)
11

Include variability with a Gamma distribution

To illustrate distribution-aware modeling, suppose the intensity X has mean μ=2 and variance v=1 and is modeled by a Gamma distribution.

The shape and scale are found from:

shape k = μ²/v = 2²/1 = 4
scale θ = v/μ = 1/2 = 0.5

For a Gamma random variable, its Laplace transform has a useful expression:

E[e−tX] = (1 + θt)−k

At miss probability t=0.4:

(1 + 0.5×0.4)−4 = 1.2−4 ≈ 0.4823 (48.23%)
Using mean only
44.93%
Using Gamma variability
48.23%
Do not misinterpret: The Gamma number is not “more accurate” merely because it differs. It is correct only if the assumed distribution adequately describes the uncertain intensity. The example shows that uncertainty can change the objective.
12

Why this relates to Kim's papers

Kim's 2023 spatial sensor-placement paper develops tractable optimization using probabilistic detection and coverage. His 2026 Gamma–Laplace paper investigates a variance-aware surrogate for stochastic target intensities.

Our Heston work gave us experience to stochastic modeling and PDE-constrained numerical approximation. It does not mean you already implemented Kim's Gamma–Laplace method.

Idea B · Step 6

Implement groundwater and sensor placement in Python

The code reproduces the hand calculations. It runs in standard Python without third-party packages.

Python · groundwater_sensors.py
from itertools import combinations
from math import exp

# Exact head solution in a two-layer medium.
def head(x):
    if 0 <= x <= 1:
        return 10 - 8*x
    if 1 < x <= 2:
        return 4 - 2*x
    raise ValueError("x must be in [0, 2]")

zones = [0.5, 1.5]
# Toy arrival law; not a validated physical relation.
intensities = [1 + 0.2*head(x) for x in zones]

# Invented sensor detection probabilities.
detect = {
    "A": [0.8, 0.2],
    "B": [0.3, 0.7],
    "C": [0.6, 0.6],
}

def coverage(selected):
    total = 0.0
    for zone_index, intensity in enumerate(intensities):
        p_miss = 1.0
        for sensor in selected:
            p_miss *= (1 - detect[sensor][zone_index])
        total += intensity * (1 - p_miss)
    return total

# Greedy: add whichever sensor improves coverage most.
selected = []
for _ in range(2):
    remaining = [s for s in detect if s not in selected]
    best = max(remaining, key=lambda s: coverage(selected + [s]))
    selected.append(best)
    print("Added:", best, "Coverage:", round(coverage(selected), 4))

print("All pairs:")
for pair in combinations(detect, 2):
    print(pair, round(coverage(pair), 4))

expected_missed = sum(intensities) - coverage(selected)
print("P(no missed targets):", round(exp(-expected_missed), 4))

# Output:
# Added: C Coverage: 2.04
# Added: A Coverage: 2.84
# ('A', 'B') 2.804
# ('A', 'C') 2.84
# ('B', 'C') 2.64
# P(no missed targets): 0.5712

Idea B · Step 7

Use what we learned from ECG

Our ECG work raises an important question: Is following physics more closely always better for the decision?

13

Recall our hybrid ECG objective

Our thesis integrated several objectives into a neural verification system: verification accuracy, reconstruction, morphology, an HDC feature, and differential-equation residuals inspired by physiological models.

Ltotal = Lverify + 0.15Lrecon + 0.05Lsmooth
+ 0.05Lmorph + 0.25LHDC + ζLphysics

We found that the no-physics variant outperformed the fully physics-regularized hybrid in ten matched runs. This shows that a physically motivated penalty can conflict with the main prediction objective.

Our implementation notes also identify an important limitation: the smoothness term used the fixed input signal and therefore had no trainable gradient. The HDC auxiliary loss's hard sign operation likewise restricted gradient flow. These are examples of why mathematical implementation details matter.

14

Transfer the question, not the ECG equation

Suppose two learned groundwater models have these hypothetical properties:

ModelPDE residualActual detection coverage
PINN 10.0012.50
PINN 20.0102.80

The model with smaller physics residual does not necessarily create the best sensor-placement decision. This is possible because the residual measures one form of model fidelity, while the operational decision depends on different quantities.

Proposed loss = PDE loss + λb · Boundary loss + λd · Decision loss

The new term would encourage the physical model to improve final sensing decisions, not only fit the governing PDE. Defining this term correctly—particularly when selecting discrete sensor locations—is an open research problem.
